# Normal Pop-Rock Bass

## Identity

A supportive, mainstream pop-rock bass accompaniment that inherits much of its rhythmic identity from the main accompaniment groove while keeping a small amount of its own phrase identity.

Use this as a normal/default family when the bass should make the arrangement feel alive and grounded without becoming the song's main hook.

Useful tags:

```text
normal
pop-rock
supportive
accompaniment-locked
melodic-support
contour-aware
eighth-note
sixteenth-connective
repetitive
low-variation
finger-bass
```

This is deliberately different from a riff-driven or hook-heavy bass style, but "supportive" does not mean "pitchless root reporter."

## Core principle

Start from the arrangement groove, then give the low line a small musical sentence of its own.

A useful hierarchy is:

```text
main accompaniment groove
        ↓
choose the important low-frequency attack positions
        ↓
establish the harmonic anchor clearly
        ↓
add a small recurring contour / pickup / approach idea
        ↓
add occasional sixteenth-note pushes or phrase-end fills
```

The bass does not need a new riff in every bar when the guitar or other main accompaniment already defines a convincing groove. It should, however, normally avoid becoming pure root-note autopilot unless that minimal behavior is intentional.

## Default melodic allowance

For ordinary accompaniment, aim for **small phrase identity rather than constant pitch movement**.

Useful low-cost devices include:

- one nearby approach note before selected chord changes;
- one recurring two-note pickup;
- a small descending or ascending contour over two or more beats;
- one neighboring tone around a repeated anchor;
- one chord-tone answer after several repeated notes;
- an occasional octave lift used as punctuation rather than a fixed loop.

A useful conceptual balance is:

```text
mostly stable harmonic foundation
+
a little connective or melodic motion
```

Do not treat any percentage as a validator target. The important question is whether the bass has a recognizable tendency without competing with the foreground melody.

## Rhythm vocabulary

A common foundation is eighth-note motion with selected sixteenth-note attacks.

Think in terms of:

```text
steady eighth-note skeleton
+
local sixteenth-note pushes
+
small intentional gaps
+
selective melodic connectors
```

Do not reduce this to mechanically sounding every quarter note.

Do not fill every sixteenth position either. The bass should inherit or simplify the accompaniment rhythm rather than compete with it.

## Repetition is allowed

Repeated pitch is not a failure.

A supportive bass may repeat the same harmonic anchor many times while rhythm carries the motion. Repetition becomes more musical when the phrase occasionally turns, approaches, answers or changes duration.

Prefer:

```text
stable repeated anchor
+ meaningful rhythmic shape
+ occasional contour event
```

over both extremes:

```text
root root root root forever
```

and

```text
constant pitch movement with no rhythmic identity
```

Change pitch because the harmony, phrase or transition gives the movement a purpose, not merely to avoid repetition.

## Relationship to the main accompaniment

Treat the main accompaniment as a strong rhythmic reference, not as a complete bass score.

Useful behaviors:

- copy or simplify important guitar/keyboard attack positions;
- reinforce the same syncopations that define the section groove;
- omit some accompaniment attacks when the low end would become too dense;
- keep the bass rhythm recognizable even when the chord root changes;
- allow the bass to add a short connective attack where the accompaniment leaves space;
- preserve one small contour idea even when the bass is rhythmically locked to the accompaniment.

The bass may be highly synchronized with the main accompaniment without duplicating every note or every attack.

## Relationship to drums

Do not force every bass note to coincide with the kick.

Use kick alignment selectively for structural weight, while allowing the bass to inherit groove from the wider accompaniment texture and retain small independent phrase motion.

A useful relationship is:

```text
important structural attacks
→ often reinforced by kick and bass together

ongoing groove attacks
→ may follow guitar/keyboard rhythm without a kick hit

small connective notes / pickups
→ may belong to the bass alone
```

## Pitch behavior

For a normal supportive part:

- establish the current harmonic anchor clearly;
- repeat it as long as the groove benefits from stability;
- add a small contour, pickup or approach gesture when there is musical space;
- use fifths, octaves or chord tones when they clarify contour or register;
- prefer nearby motion for ordinary connections;
- reserve obvious fills for phrase or section boundaries;
- let one tiny melodic device recur so the part sounds like one player rather than independently generated bars.

Do not make `root-fifth-octave-root` a fixed loop. Those intervals are options, not a pattern generator.

## Note length and articulation

Tie note length to the rhythmic role rather than one fixed gate ratio.

For example:

```text
stable anchor
→ may occupy most of its available interval or sustain longer

connective eighth
→ usually occupies enough of the interval to remain part of the phrase

quick pickup / sixteenth
→ may be shorter and more articulated
```

Leave a small release gap when the style needs clear pop-rock articulation, but avoid large empty holes between every note.

The goal is:

```text
connected phrase with visible articulation
```

not fully legato sustain and not isolated MIDI dots.

## Section behavior

Keep the same bass identity across a section unless the arrangement actually changes.

Possible section transformations include:

- quiet verse: mostly anchors with one recurring pickup or approach idea;
- normal verse: stable groove plus modest contour;
- pre-chorus: increase directional motion before arrival rather than merely adding notes everywhere;
- chorus: reinforce a stronger pulse while preserving one small melodic identity;
- phrase ending: add one short transition or fill;
- breakdown: use longer sustained anchors and reduce melodic information deliberately.

Section lift does not require more pitch complexity. The bass may become more memorable through contour, register or timing without becoming busier.

## Variation policy

Use low-cost variations first:

- add or remove one sixteenth attack;
- shorten or lengthen one repeated note;
- add one approach note before a chord change;
- repeat a familiar pickup at a new harmonic anchor;
- omit one repeated attack under a vocal accent;
- alter one phrase-end contour;
- simplify back to the stable pattern afterward.

Keep the groove and contour identity more stable than the individual bar details.

## Failure modes

Revise when:

- the bass plays one detached root note every beat by default;
- repeated roots continue for long spans with no deliberate phrase identity even when the arrangement leaves room;
- every bar invents a new bass rhythm or contour;
- pitch changes are added only to avoid repetition;
- every bass onset is forced to copy the kick;
- the bass ignores the main accompaniment groove;
- every note has the same arbitrary gate length;
- root/fifth/octave becomes a mechanical repeating formula;
- fills or passing notes appear constantly instead of serving phrase direction;
- the bass becomes more melodic than the foreground role requires.

## Pairing

Pair naturally with:

- `smooth-melodic-support-bass` when the default line should become more flowing, rounded and connective;
- `groove-motif-bass` when the section needs a stronger recurring rhythmic/melodic identity;
- `section-linked-pop-rock-bass` when the bass must change density and behavior with distinct rhythm-section roles.

Borrow selectively. A normal pop-rock section may take one motif or approach device without importing another Material's full density or articulation behavior.

## Study provenance

This material was abstracted from a user-provided MIDI study of a mainstream pop-rock arrangement, then revised after later bass studies demonstrated that supportive bass can retain a small melodic identity without becoming a foreground riff.

Observed in the original studied bass track:

- the MIDI used GM program 33, Electric Bass (finger);
- the track contained 735 note attacks;
- 607 adjacent attacks repeated the same pitch, about 83%, showing that repeated pitch can coexist with an active groove;
- the dominant attack gaps were 0.50 beat (445 occurrences) and 0.25 beat (181 occurrences), supporting an eighth-note skeleton with selected sixteenth-note motion;
- many full bars used about 9-10 bass attacks, and one stable rhythmic identity recurred across a large number of bars;
- when the main acoustic-guitar strums were grouped and compared on a sixteenth grid, bass attack positions strongly overlapped the main accompaniment rhythm in ordinary sections;
- exact bass/kick coincidence was much lower than bass/main-accompaniment rhythmic overlap, so this source does not support treating the bass as a simple copy of the kick;
- notes following 0.50-beat spacing commonly occupied most of that interval, while 0.25-beat attacks used correspondingly shorter durations, leaving small articulation gaps.

Later user-provided smooth/groove bass references support adding a small contour or motif allowance as a general compositional option, but their exact statistics are not imported into this Material.

The source pitches, chord progressions, section sequences and full rhythmic masks remain source-specific.

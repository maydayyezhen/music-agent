# Normal Pop-Rock Bass

## Identity

A supportive, mainstream pop-rock bass accompaniment that inherits much of its rhythmic identity from the main accompaniment groove instead of inventing a separate bass riff in every bar.

Use this as a normal/default family when the bass should make the arrangement feel alive and grounded without becoming the song's main hook.

Useful tags:

```text
normal
pop-rock
supportive
accompaniment-locked
eighth-note
sixteenth-connective
repetitive
low-variation
finger-bass
```

This is deliberately different from a riff-driven or hook-heavy bass style.

## Core principle

Start from the arrangement groove, not from a rule such as `one root note per beat`.

A useful hierarchy is:

```text
main accompaniment groove
        ↓
choose the important low-frequency attack positions
        ↓
repeat the current harmonic anchor confidently
        ↓
add occasional sixteenth-note pushes, transitions or fills
```

The bass does not need a new rhythmic idea when the guitar or other main accompaniment already defines a convincing groove.

## Rhythm vocabulary

A common foundation is eighth-note motion with selected sixteenth-note attacks.

Think in terms of:

```text
steady eighth-note skeleton
+
local sixteenth-note pushes
+
small intentional gaps
```

Do not reduce this to mechanically sounding every quarter note.

Do not fill every sixteenth position either. The bass should inherit or simplify the accompaniment rhythm rather than compete with it.

## Repetition is allowed

Repeated pitch is not a failure.

A supportive bass may repeat the same harmonic anchor many times while the rhythm carries the motion.

Prefer:

```text
stable repeated pitch with a good groove
```

over:

```text
constant pitch movement with no rhythmic identity
```

Change pitch because the harmony, phrase or transition calls for it, not merely to avoid repetition.

## Relationship to the main accompaniment

Treat the main accompaniment as a strong rhythmic reference.

Useful behaviors:

- copy or simplify important guitar/keyboard attack positions;
- reinforce the same syncopations that define the section groove;
- omit some accompaniment attacks when the low end would become too dense;
- keep the bass rhythm recognizable even when the chord root changes;
- allow the bass to add a short connective attack where the accompaniment leaves space.

The bass may be highly synchronized with the main accompaniment without duplicating every note or every attack.

## Relationship to drums

Do not force every bass note to coincide with the kick.

Use kick alignment selectively for structural weight, while allowing the bass to inherit groove from the wider accompaniment texture.

A useful relationship is:

```text
important structural attacks
→ often reinforced by kick and bass together

ongoing groove attacks
→ may follow guitar/keyboard rhythm without a kick hit

small connective notes
→ may belong to the bass alone
```

## Pitch behavior

For a normal supportive part:

- establish the current harmonic anchor clearly;
- repeat it as long as the groove benefits from stability;
- use fifths, octaves or chord tones sparingly when they clarify contour;
- use short approach or passing notes near transitions rather than continuously walking;
- reserve obvious fills for phrase or section boundaries.

Do not make `root-fifth-octave-root` a fixed loop. Those intervals are options, not a pattern generator.

## Note length and articulation

Tie note length to the rhythmic subdivision.

For example:

```text
eighth-note attack
→ note may occupy most of the eighth-note interval

sixteenth-note attack
→ note may occupy most of the sixteenth-note interval
```

Leave a small release gap when the style needs clear pop-rock articulation, but avoid large empty holes between every note.

The goal is:

```text
connected pulse with visible articulation
```

not fully legato sustain and not isolated MIDI dots.

## Section behavior

Keep the same bass identity across a section unless the arrangement actually changes.

Possible section transformations include:

- verse: fewer attacks or more repeated anchor notes;
- pre-chorus: add selected sixteenth pushes or reduce empty space;
- chorus: reinforce a stronger eighth-note pulse;
- phrase ending: add one short transition or fill;
- breakdown: use longer sustained anchors instead of constant pulse.

Section lift does not require more pitch complexity.

## Variation policy

Use low-cost variations first:

- add or remove one sixteenth attack;
- shorten or lengthen one repeated note;
- add one approach note before a chord change;
- omit one repeated attack under a vocal accent;
- add one phrase-end fill;
- simplify back to the stable pattern afterward.

Keep the groove identity more stable than the individual bar details.

## Failure modes

Revise when:

- the bass plays one detached root note every beat;
- every bar invents a new bass rhythm;
- pitch changes are added only to avoid repetition;
- every bass onset is forced to copy the kick;
- the bass ignores the main accompaniment groove;
- every note has the same arbitrary gate length;
- root/fifth/octave becomes a mechanical repeating formula;
- fills appear constantly instead of at meaningful phrase points.

## Study provenance

This material was abstracted from a user-provided MIDI study of a mainstream pop-rock arrangement.

Observed in the studied bass track:

- the MIDI used GM program 33, Electric Bass (finger);
- the track contained 735 note attacks;
- 607 adjacent attacks repeated the same pitch, about 83%, showing that repeated pitch can coexist with an active groove;
- the dominant attack gaps were 0.50 beat (445 occurrences) and 0.25 beat (181 occurrences), supporting an eighth-note skeleton with selected sixteenth-note motion;
- many full bars used about 9-10 bass attacks, and one stable rhythmic identity recurred across a large number of bars;
- when the main acoustic-guitar strums were grouped and compared on a sixteenth grid, bass attack positions strongly overlapped the main accompaniment rhythm in ordinary sections;
- exact bass/kick coincidence was much lower than bass/main-accompaniment rhythmic overlap, so this source does not support treating the bass as a simple copy of the kick;
- notes following 0.50-beat spacing commonly occupied most of that interval, while 0.25-beat attacks used correspondingly shorter durations, leaving small articulation gaps.

These observations support a normal accompaniment-locked pop-rock bass family. The source's exact pitches, chord progression, section sequence and full rhythmic masks are intentionally omitted.
